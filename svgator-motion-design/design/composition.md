# Composition — the still frame has to be good before it moves

Motion cannot rescue a weak drawing. A logo that's badly proportioned, an icon with no focal point, a scene where everything competes — animating it just makes the problems move. Design the still first: get the composition, the hierarchy, and the negative space right, then choreograph on top of a frame that already reads. This is the graphic-design half of the craft; reach for it whenever you are *drawing* the artwork, not just when it's handed to you.

## The squint test — the fastest read on hierarchy

Blur your eyes at the static frame (or imagine it at 10% size). You should still be able to name, in order:

1. the one thing the eye lands on first,
2. the second thing,
3. the clear groupings.

If everything smears into the same weight, the composition has no hierarchy — and motion will only spread the confusion. Fix the still before you keyframe.

## Hierarchy comes from several dimensions at once, never size alone

Amateur work leans on size and nothing else. Designed work stacks two or three signals on the *same* element so the priority is unmistakable.

| Tool | Strong | Weak |
|------|--------|------|
| Size | ≥3:1 ratio between primary and secondary | <2:1 — reads as "roughly equal" |
| Weight | bold vs regular, thick vs hairline stroke | one step apart |
| Color | high contrast / the single accent | similar tones |
| Position | top / left, optical center | bottom / right, edges |
| Space | surrounded by air | crowded in |

The hero of an icon should be **larger, heavier, AND more isolated** — not just bigger. That stacking is what the motion layer then amplifies (the hero enters first and biggest → see [../director/choreography.md](../director/choreography.md)).

## One hero. Give the eye somewhere to land

Every composition needs a single dominant element. Two things fighting for first place means neither wins. Decide the hero, commit real contrast to it, and demote everything else to support. This is the same element that will carry the primary motion — the still and the motion agree on where attention goes.

## Negative space is a material, not leftover

The empty area is doing as much work as the marks. Crowding to fill the canvas is the tell of an untrained eye. Let the hero breathe; a mark with air around it reads as important for free. Tighten *related* elements into groups and open *generous* gaps between groups — that rhythm of tight-then-loose is what makes a layout feel composed rather than sprinkled. Uniform spacing everywhere reads as monotonous.

## Break the center. Asymmetry reads as designed

Dead-center everything is the safe reflex, and it looks like the reflex. Unless the piece is a deliberately symmetric mark (a radial badge, a mandala loader), push for:

- an off-center focal point balanced by a smaller counterweight of space or a secondary mark,
- weight to one side, air to the other,
- an intentional grid-break — one element that steps out of alignment *on purpose* to draw the eye.

Balance is not symmetry. A big shape on the left can be balanced by a lot of empty space on the right. That tension is more interesting than a mirror.

## Proportion and scale — pick ratios, don't eyeball

Relationships between element sizes should feel intentional, not accidental. Reuse a small set of ratios (say ~1.5× or ~2× steps) so sizes look like a system. Two elements *almost* the same size looks like a mistake; make them clearly equal or clearly different. For lettering inside a mark, keep the wordmark wide and open rather than cramped and tall — narrow, many-line type is the single most common composition failure in a logo.

## Optical adjustment — trust the eye over the math

Geometric centering and true visual centering are not the same thing:

- A triangle/play glyph centered by its bounding box looks shoved left — nudge it right toward its visual mass.
- Arrows want to sit slightly toward the direction they point.
- A circle next to a square of the "same" size looks smaller — oversize the circle a hair so they read as equal (optical sizing).
- Text aligned to a hard edge looks indented because of the letterform's side bearing — pull it out a touch.

These micro-corrections are invisible individually and unmistakable in aggregate — the same principle that governs the motion layer's unseen details.

## The order of operations

1. **Silhouette** — is the overall shape readable and distinctive at a glance and at size?
2. **Hierarchy** — squint test passes; one clear hero, clear groups.
3. **Space** — tight groups, generous gaps, the hero breathing.
4. **Balance** — off-center but resolved; nothing accidentally symmetric or accidentally lopsided.
5. **Optical pass** — nudge for visual, not geometric, alignment.

Only once the frozen frame survives all five do you move to color ([color.md](color.md)), form and lettering ([form-and-type.md](form-and-type.md)), and then motion.
